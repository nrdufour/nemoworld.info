# Title

"Let's Be Honest About AI, This Time"

"This time" is a promissory note: it points back at the last round of this
argument, when the vocabulary was rigged on purpose. Sections 5 and 6 are what
cash it. If the history does not land, the title over-promises.

Consequence: section 5 is a FULL section, not a boxed aside.

In reserve:
- Neither good nor bad
- There is no pro-AI, only who controls it
- Pro and anti are not positions

Thesis in one line: *being pro or anti a technology is a category error — a
technology is not a moral subject.* The real axis is **control**: who owns it,
who operates it, who pays for its errors, who captures the gain, who can refuse.

## 3. The category error

ROLE: the analytical first blow.

Material: Kranzberg's First Law (see Sources).
Land this: "pro" and "anti" name nothing, because a technology is not a moral
subject. And the second clause — *nor is it neutral* — blocks the escape into
indifference. You cannot abstain; you have to go down a level.

## 4. Technology comes in packages

ROLE: the argument that defeats both camps at once, without a moral claim.

Material: Kranzberg's Third Law.
Land this: both camps pretend a bundle is separable. The pro wants the tool
without the package. The anti denies the tool in order to refuse the package.

## 5. The word "luddite"

ROLE: the historical piece, and the proof that the vocabulary was built on
purpose to hide the question of control.

Points to land:
- skilled workers, specific machines — not machine-haters
- petitions and regulation first; escalation came after combination was made
  illegal and Parliament refused
- the label was manufactured afterwards: a wage-and-control dispute reframed as
  a belief dispute, so nobody had to discuss who owned the frames
- the trade was destroyed within a generation; the productivity gain was real
  and the workers captured none of it

## Sources

Melvin Kranzberg, "Technology and History: 'Kranzberg's Laws'", *Technology and
Culture* 27, no. 3 (July 1986). First Law: "Technology is neither good nor bad;
nor is it neutral." Third Law: "Technology comes in packages, big and small."

E. J. Hobsbawm, "The Machine Breakers", *Past & Present*, no. 1 (February 1952):
57–70. doi:10.1093/past/1.1.57 — the essay that corrected the record on who the
machine-breakers were and what they actually wanted.

Kevin Binfield, ed., *Writings of the Luddites* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins
University Press, 2004) — the primary source: addresses, petitions and letters
written by Luddites and sympathisers between 1811 and 1816. Their own words.

Brian Merchant, *Blood in the Machine* (2023) — the recent, widely read account;
useful for the modern replay as much as for the history.

Gavin Mueller, *Breaking Things at Work: The Luddites Were Right About Why You
Hate Your Job* (Verso, 2021) — the contemporary restatement of the argument.

Legal frame: the Frame Breaking Act 1812 made machine-breaking a capital
offence; the Combination Acts (1799–1800) had already made combination illegal.
